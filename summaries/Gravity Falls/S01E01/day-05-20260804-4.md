# Day 5 - Tourist Trapped: two patterns in one sentence

Date: 2026-08-04 (round 4) | Source: Gravity Falls S01E01 Tourist Trapped | Level: Tier 1+

## Today's Practice

Ten longer sentences that each carry two patterns: hedging, promises,
threats, payback, and questions. This was the strongest round: all ten were
answered, and eight of ten were natural or needed only a small fix. The two
major fixes both came from changing the speaker's stance, turning a
frustrated effort into a plan and a hurt question into a request.

## Corrections Worth Remembering

### 6. Stance: effort is not a plan

Original: "I'm trying to tell you that Norman is not what he seems!"
My version: "I'm gonna tell you that Norman is not what he looks like!"
Model: "I keep telling you Norman isn't what he seems!"

"Trying to tell you" carries frustration: the speaker has been attempting to
explain. "Gonna tell you" is a future plan, which changes the situation.
"Seems" is about appearance and behavior; "looks like" is only physical.

### 7. A hurt question is not a request

Original: "What about me, huh? Why can't you trust me?"
My version: "How about me, huh? Please trust me."
Model: "What about me? Why won't you trust me?"

The original is a wounded, angry question. "Please trust me" turns it into a
polite request and removes the hurt. Keep the question form and the emotion.

### 5. The fixed phrase: "That's what you get for..."

Original: "That's for lying to me!"
My version: "It's for lying to me!"
Model: "That's what you get for lying to me!"

"It" has nothing to point at, so the sentence floats. English has a ready
phrase for punishment-after-the-fact: "That's what you get for...".

## Patterns I Can Use Today

- "I think I can be a little suspicious sometimes."
- "I should tell you something."
- "That's what you get for lying to me!"
- "Be careful not to hit any walkers."
- "What about me? Why won't you trust me?"

## How This Builds Speaking

This round moved from words to stance: the two major fixes failed because the
rephrase changed how the speaker felt, not just the words. In speech, the
stance is what the listener hears first, so match the emotion before the
vocabulary. Follow up by reading the model versions aloud, especially the
two fixed phrases.
