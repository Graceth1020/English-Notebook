## Corrections Worth Remembering

### 6. "Bring" is not the offer

Original: "Dude, you could totally use my boat for your hunt."
My version: "Dude, you could totally bring my boat for your hunt."
Model: "Dude, you could totally use my boat for your hunt."

"Bring" means carry something to a place, so "bring my boat" would mean the
boat moves with you. The offer is to let the other person use or borrow the
boat, which is what "use" says.

### 5. A verdict, not a location statement

Original: "We got ourselves a crazy old man."
My version: "There's a crazy old man here."
Model: "We got ourselves a crazy old man."

"Got ourselves a ..." is a casual verdict: the kids have ended up with a
crazy old man and are commenting on it. "There's ... here" just says he is
in the room, which drops the whole point of the line.

### 2. "Anything" only in negatives and questions

Original: "Blindfolds never lead to anything good."
My version: "Blindfolds always cause anything bad."
Model: "Blindfolds never end well."

After an affirmative "always", English needs "something bad", not "anything
bad", and "cause anything bad" is not a collocation a native speaker would
use. The most natural swap for "never lead to anything good" is "never end
well".

## Common Phrases and Fixed Structures

- "Then you know what ... means!": "Then you know what this means!"
- "Never lead to anything good" / "never end well": "Blindfolds never end well."
- "I feel like + clause": "I feel like all my other senses are heightened."
- "I'll show you how + clause": "I'll show you how a real man fishes!"
- "Got ourselves a + noun": "We got ourselves a crazy old man."
- "Could totally + verb": "Dude, you could totally use my boat."
- "Permission to + verb?": "Permission to taste some?"
- Dramatic dismissal: "Traitors! I'll find my own fishin' buddies."
- Playful insult "you rapscallion": "Zeus, you rapscallion!"
- "In my day": "I made lots of robots in my day."

## How This Builds Speaking

After Day 9's clean round, Day 10 shows where the hard block still bites:
the exact verb and the fixed phrase carry the meaning, and swapping in a
plainer word ("bring" for "use", "approval" for "permission", "during my
life" for "in my day") changes or flattens the line. Read the model versions
aloud three times, then plant "got ourselves a ..." and "permission to ..."
into two sentences of your own before the next round.
