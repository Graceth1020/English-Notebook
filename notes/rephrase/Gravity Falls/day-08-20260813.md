- **Word**: realize
- **Definition**: To become aware of something or understand it clearly for the first time; also, to achieve or make real something you have wanted or planned (realize a dream, realize potential).
- **Usage**: Verb, transitive: realize that + clause (I realized that I was wrong), realize + wh-clause (I realized what happened), realize + noun (realize a dream/goal/potential). In the past with 'didn't realize' it often introduces a sudden understanding. British spelling: realise. In the day's sentence 7, the closely related adjective 'real' is used in the realization pattern 'I guess the real + noun + is ...'.
- **Examples**:
    - I didn't realize it was so late.
    - She finally realized her dream of becoming a pilot.
    - He realized the mistake as soon as he spoke.
    - It took me a while to realize the truth.
- **Synonyms**: notice, recognize, understand, grasp, become aware of; also achieve, fulfill, attain
- **Antonyms**: miss, overlook, misunderstand, ignore; also abandon, give up

- Sentence: Well, I run away from nothing... Nothing except for when I ran away from my annoying Grunkle Stan who I ditched in order to pursue that lake monster.
- Structure: Compound-complex sentence built on a contrast. Part 1 (main clause): 'I run away from nothing'; echo emphasis: 'Nothing'; Part 2 (contrast): preposition 'except for' + subordinate 'when' clause 'when I ran away from my annoying Grunkle Stan' + nonrestrictive relative clause 'who I ditched' + purpose infinitive 'in order to pursue that lake monster'.
- Translation: 嗯，我什么都不逃避……除了当我逃离我烦人的斯坦叔公的时候，为了追那个湖怪我把他甩了。
- Notes: Discourse marker: 'Well' opens the turn. Main clause in present simple ('run away from nothing' = fear nothing, be afraid of nothing; 'run away from' is a phrasal verb). 'Nothing' is repeated for emphasis, then rephrased by 'except for when ...': 'except for' + 'when' clause names the single exception. Inside the 'when' clause: subject 'I', verb 'ran away from' (past simple of the phrasal verb), object 'my annoying Grunkle Stan' (Grunkle = casual family coinage for great-uncle). Relative clause 'who I ditched': 'who' functions as the object of 'ditch' (informal; 'whom' would be the formal object form), giving 'Stan, whom I ditched'. Purpose infinitive: 'in order to pursue that lake monster' explains why he ditched Stan; 'in order to' + base verb = a more explicit way of saying 'to'.
