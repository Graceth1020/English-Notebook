- Sentence 1: I got the message yesterday.
- Sentence 2: I have gotten the message.
- Question: What is the difference between got and gotten?
- Answer: In American English, got is the simple past and gotten is the past participle; in British English, got is normally both forms. Have got is also a separate idiom for possession or obligation.
- Reasoning: American English uses get-got-gotten for completed actions after have/has/had, as in I have gotten back to them. British English normally uses get-got-got, as in I have got back to them. The form have got can also mean possess or must, as in I have got time and I have got to go; those are idiomatic uses, not ordinary present perfect statements.

- Sentence 1: We figured out if it was possible.
- Sentence 2: We figured out whether it was possible.
- Question: Why use whether after figure out?
- Answer: Whether is the clearer choice for an embedded yes-no question. If is often possible in informal English, but whether makes the possible-or-not meaning explicit and avoids confusion when another if introduces a condition.
- Reasoning: Figure out can introduce an embedded question about two alternatives: whether something was possible or not. In the original sentence, the first if asked whether booking offline was possible, while the second if introduced the condition if it had been booked online. Using whether for the first clause separates the question from the condition. Whether is also required in some structures, such as after a preposition, before an infinitive, and with or not. If remains correct for the conditional clause.
