- **Input**: 当提到什么事情时（当谈及／说到某个话题时）
- **Expressions**: when it comes to + noun/gerund / when it comes down to it / speaking of X / talking of which / on the subject of X / as for X / in terms of X
- **Register**: when it comes to (neutral, THE default frame for switching to a topic and judging ability/quality) - when it comes down to it (casual, slightly different meaning: "at the end of the day") - speaking of X (casual, only for linking to what was JUST said) - talking of which (casual, British-leaning, same linking use) - on the subject of X (slightly formal, deliberate topic switch) - as for X (neutral, marks contrast or a new sub-topic, often mildly dismissive) - in terms of X (slightly formal, common in meetings and presentations)
- **Examples**:
  - When it comes to logging, we're still a mess.
  - When it comes down to it, cost is what matters.
  - Speaking of tests, did you run the suite?
  - Talking of which, have you seen the new dashboard?
  - On the subject of monitoring, we should set up alerts.
  - As for the deployment, that can wait.
  - In terms of performance, this is a big win.
  - I'm pretty good with SQL, but when it comes to regex I give up.
- **Avoid**: "when mention something" - ungrammatical calque of 当提到. | "when we mention X" - grammatical but means something else: it describes the act of mentioning, not the topic frame; use it only for timing ("when we mention this to the client, phrase it carefully"). | "when talking about X" - common among Chinese speakers; not wrong, but natives strongly prefer "when it comes to X" for the topic-judgment meaning, so the talk version often sounds translated. | "as to X" - formal and slightly starchy in AmE speech; fine in legal/formal writing. | "with regard to" - email/bureaucratic register, heavy in speech. | "regarding X, ..." - same: written register, stiff when spoken.
- **Notes**: CORE FRAME: "when it comes to" is the default for “说到/谈及某个领域”, and it is almost always followed by an evaluation: "when it comes to speed, nothing beats it". | "speaking of X" is NOT a free topic-switcher - it only works when X was just mentioned or is clearly in the air; using it cold sounds odd. | DON'T CONFUSE: "when it comes to X" (topic) vs "when it comes down to it" (= bottom line / 说到底) - one word changes the meaning completely. | Grammar: "when it comes to + noun or -ing", the "to" is a preposition, so "when it comes to testing", never "when it comes to test". | Flow trick: front the frame to buy thinking time - "Well, when it comes to monitoring, I mean, we basically have nothing." | Reductions: "speaking of" often becomes "speakin' a"; "when it comes to" runs as "when id-cumz-duh". | "as for" at the start of a reply often signals “至于这个嘛...” with a hint of distance: "As for the timeline, don't ask me."

- **Input**: 约定好做某事（双方说好了要做某事）
- **Expressions**: agreed to do X / we said we'd X / made plans to X / set up a meeting/call / be supposed to do X / be scheduled to do X / be locked in for X / agreed on doing X
- **Register**: agreed to do X (neutral, the core meaning: both sides committed) - we said we'd X (casual, the most common way to REPORT an agreement in speech) - made plans to X (casual, softer, often social) - set up a call/meeting (neutral, the default verb for arranging work events) - be supposed to do X (casual, extremely high frequency; means "as was agreed/planned") - be scheduled to do X (slightly formal, official timelines) - be locked in for X (very casual, confirms nothing will change) - agreed on doing X (neutral, stresses the consensus reached, not the commitment)
- **Examples**:
  - We agreed to meet on Friday and hash it out.
  - We said we'd review the PR before the release.
  - We made plans to sync up next week.
  - I set up a call with the vendor for Thursday.
  - We're supposed to demo it at the all-hands.
  - The migration is scheduled to start on Monday.
  - We're locked in for next Tuesday.
  - We agreed on shipping the beta first.
- **Avoid**: "appoint to do something" - "appoint" means to assign a person to a position ("appointed him as lead"), it never means 约定. | "make an appointment to do X" - only for formal service appointments (doctor, dentist, embassy), not for agreements with colleagues or friends. | "we scheduled to meet" - "schedule" as a verb needs an object: "we scheduled a meeting" ✓; for the commitment use "we're scheduled to meet". | "we have an agreement to do X" - legal/contract register, absurd in casual speech. | "promised to do X" - stronger than 约定: a promise, not a mutual plan; overusing it sounds dramatic. | "ordered to do X" - wrong word entirely; that means 命令.
- **Notes**: TRIPLE CONTRAST with agree: "agree to do X" = commit to an action ("we agreed to ship Friday"); "agree on X / doing X" = reach consensus ("we agreed on shipping the beta first"); "agree with someone" = concur with an opinion ("I agree with you"). | "be supposed to" is the highest-frequency spoken way to talk about what was agreed/planned: "we're supposed to demo it" implies an existing arrangement. It also implies mild obligation, so it is the natural way to push back: "weren't we supposed to ship today?" | Pronunciation: "supposed to" reduces to "s'posed to" / "supposta" (/səˈpoʊstə/) - saying the full two syllables of "supposed" sounds overly careful. | "we said we'd X" - reported speech is how natives usually convey 约定好 in conversation; "'d" = would, and it reduces hard. | "set up" handles the ARRANGEMENT, "agreed to" handles the COMMITMENT - use them together: "I set up the call; we agreed to decide on Friday." | "locked in" = finalized, won't change: "plans are locked in"; great for confirming, avoid in formal email. | For cancelling a 约定: "rain check" (postpone a social plan) and "back out of" (withdraw from a commitment, negative tone).
