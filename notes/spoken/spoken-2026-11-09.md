## 2026-11-09 18:13
- **Input**: 表达在XX情况下
- **Expressions**: In that case / If that's the case / When it comes to XX / Depending on XX / As far as XX goes / Given XX
- **Register**: neutral; the first two respond to a scenario someone just described, the rest frame a topic or condition
- **Examples**:
  - The client wants it by Friday. In that case, we need to start today.
  - If that's the case, we should double-check the numbers before we send anything.
  - When it comes to pricing, we're actually the cheapest in the market.
  - Depending on traffic, we might be a little late.
  - As far as the budget goes, we're fine for now.
  - Given the circumstances, I think we should postpone the launch.
- **Avoid**: 「In the case of XX」 — grammatically fine but sounds like a contract or an academic paper, not speech. 「Under the situation of XX」 — a direct calque from 在这种情况下, no native speaker says it. 「In the circumstance of XX」 — non-native and unnatural. 「Regarding the situation of XX」 — bureaucratic filler.
- **Notes**: Biggest trap: 「in case」 alone means 以防 (to be safe), not 在XX情况下 — 「In case it rains, bring an umbrella」 is a precaution, while 「In that case」 is 那样的话. 「When it comes to」 takes a noun or gerund, never a full clause: 「when it comes to pricing」 is right, 「when it comes to we price」 is wrong. 「As far as X goes」 is casual and usually narrows scope or shifts topic. 「Given X」 works as a preposition-like opener and is very common in both speech and semi-formal writing. BrE prefers 「in the event that」 in formal registers where AmE would just say 「if」.

## 2026-11-09 18:16
- **Input**: 表达在我不熟悉这个项目又需要保证交付质量的情况下
- **Expressions**: Even though I'm new to this project, I still need to make sure we ship something solid / I've never worked on this project, but the quality still has to hold up / I'm walking into this cold, so I've got to be extra careful / Given that I'm not up to speed, I still have to keep the quality bar up / New to the project or not, the quality bar doesn't move / I'm still getting up to speed, but I can't let quality slip
- **Register**: neutral to casual; all six fit a normal standup or one-on-one with a manager, the given-that version is the most formal of the set
- **Examples**:
  - Even though I'm new to this project, I still need to make sure we ship something solid.
  - I've never worked on this project, but the quality still has to hold up.
  - I'm walking into this cold, so I've got to be extra careful about quality.
  - Given that I'm not up to speed on the project yet, I still have to keep the quality bar up.
  - New to the project or not, the quality bar doesn't move.
  - I'm still getting up to speed on this project, but I can't let quality slip.
- **Avoid**: 「Under the circumstances that I am not familiar with the project and need to guarantee delivery quality」 — stacks a that-clause onto a preposition, a direct calque that no native speaker builds. 「In the case where I am unfamiliar with the project yet must ensure delivery quality」 — legal or academic register, not speech. 「Although I am not acquainted with the project, I must nonetheless guarantee the quality of delivery」 — reads like a written memo, and 「acquainted with」 sounds stiff in conversation. 「Delivery quality」 itself is the giveaway — native speakers say 「quality」 or 「what we ship」, not a logistics-style compound.
- **Notes**: The core frame is [even though / but / given that] + unfamiliarity, I still + quality duty. The 「still」 is the load-bearing word: drop it and the sentence becomes two unrelated facts instead of two constraints pulling against each other. 「Walking into it cold」 means with zero prior context, casual and mostly workplace or tech talk. 「Up to speed」 is the standard workplace idiom for being familiar with a project — far more common than 「familiar with」. 「Quality bar」 is metonymic workplace shorthand (set the bar, raise the bar, the bar does not move). For the second half, 「can't let quality slip」 and 「let it slide」 are the natural spoken forms of 保证质量. BrE leans on 「ensure」 where AmE says 「make sure」.
