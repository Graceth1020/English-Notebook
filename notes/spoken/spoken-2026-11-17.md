- **Input**: 形容商品非常紧张、很容易卖完（比如机票很紧张，一下子就卖完了）
- **Expressions**: They're going fast / It books up fast / They sell out in minutes / Selling like crazy / Flying off the shelves / There's huge demand
- **Register**: neutral to casual; the first three are the everyday way to warn someone, the last three lean more idiomatic or business-like
- **Examples**:
  - Those flights are going fast, I'd book today if I were you.
  - That route books up fast around the holidays.
  - The Friday show sold out in minutes.
  - Their new drop is selling like crazy.
  - The limited edition ones are flying off the shelves.
  - There's huge demand for that seat class right now.
- **Avoid**: 「The commodity is very tight」 - a literal calque; 「tight」 only describes supply in business-news jargon, never a consumer product in speech. 「It is easy to be sold out」 - passive calque, no native speaker phrases it that way. 「The tickets are very nervous」 - mistranslation of 紧张. 「Selling like hotcakes」 - understandable but dated, it sounds like a 1950s advertisement.
- **Notes**: The trap word is 紧张: in English 「tight」 works for supply in news register (「supply is tight」), never for a product on the shelf. For consumers the plainest warning is 「going fast」 in present continuous. 「Books up」 is the right verb for flights, hotels, restaurants and appointments; physical goods instead 「sell out」 or 「fly off the shelves」. 「Flying off the shelves」 is a fixed phrase - keep the plural and the article. 「Selling like crazy」 is the modern stand-in for 「selling like hotcakes」. 「Snapped up」 (BrE-leaning) means bought instantly: 「They got snapped up in an hour」. Softer semi-formal versions are 「in short supply」 and 「hard to get」.

- **Input**: 描述赞同某个人的观点、行为之类的
- **Expressions**: I'm with you / Exactly / That makes sense / That's a good point / I'm on board / Couldn't agree more
- **Register**: neutral to very casual; all six fit a normal US work conversation, the two one-word ones are the most casual
- **Examples**:
  - I'm with you on this, let's push it through.
  - Exactly, that's what I've been saying all along.
  - That makes sense, I hadn't thought about the cost angle.
  - That's a good point, we should probably revisit the timeline.
  - I'm on board with the new process, it'll save us a lot of back-and-forth.
  - Couldn't agree more, the docs need a rewrite.
- **Avoid**: 「I agree with your opinion」 - redundant and stiff; natives just say 「I agree」 or 「I'm with you」. 「I am in agreement with you」 - bureaucratese, sounds like a memo. 「I concur」 - legal register, almost nobody says it aloud socially. 「I approve of your behavior」 - this sounds like a manager grading someone; for 赞同行为 natives say 「Good call」 or 「That's the right call」. 「I hear you」 - means I registered what you said, not I agree, the single biggest trap for Chinese speakers.
- **Notes**: 「I'm with you」 is the highest-frequency spoken agreement marker and covers both opinions and plans; 「I'm with you on X」 pins the specific point. 「Exactly」 and 「Totally」 are one-word backchannels: extremely common, but minimal, so they carry little weight - add a clause if you want to sound considered. Subtle difference: 「That makes sense」 implies you were just persuaded, while 「I agree」 implies you were already on side. 「That's a good point」 credits the reasoning rather than the conclusion, which is the polite way to concede in a meeting. 「I'm on board」 is for plans and actions, never for an abstract opinion. 「Couldn't agree more」 is emphatic and slightly set-piece; 「couldn't agree less」 is a deliberate joke, not standard. 「Fair enough」 is qualified agreement - OK, I accept that - not full agreement. 「Hear, hear」 is BrE parliamentary and often ironic.

- **Input**: 用来表达 XX 是合理的
- **Expressions**: That makes sense / That's fair / Makes sense to me / That's reasonable / Can't argue with that / There's a case for it
- **Register**: neutral to casual; the first four are everyday verdicts, the last two lean hedged or slightly formal
- **Examples**:
  - That makes sense, given how tight the timeline is.
  - That's fair, they did give us plenty of notice.
  - Makes sense to me, I'd probably do the same thing.
  - That's reasonable, I don't think anyone would push back on it.
  - Can't argue with that, the numbers speak for themselves.
  - There's a case for it, though I'd want to see the cost first.
- **Avoid**: 「It is reasonable that XX」 - the extraposition is fine in writing but sounds formal out loud. 「It is rational」 - 合理 is not rational in everyday speech; rational belongs to philosophy and economics and sounds clinical when applied to a person decision. 「It is quite logical」 - a non-native flavour; natives say 「that makes sense」. 「There is rationality in it」 and 「It is justified to say」 - direct calques, no native builds these.
- **Notes**: 合理 splits into three English words depending on what is being judged: 「fair」 for rules, prices, treatment and decisions that affect people; 「reasonable」 for plans, requests and expectations; 「makes sense」 for logic, explanations and choices. Picking the wrong one is the usual non-native tell. 「That's fair」 is the standard reply when someone hands you a rule or a no - it accepts without pretending to like it. 「That tracks」 is casual and generational (younger and tech circles), meaning consistent with what I already know. 「Fair enough」 concedes with a hint of reservation, weaker than 「that's fair」. 「Can't argue with that」 is stronger, close to I have no objection. 「There's a case for it」 hedges - you see merit but are not endorsing. For concerns and points, 「valid」 is the common adjective: 「that's a valid concern」. BrE leans on 「fair enough」 where AmE says 「that's fair」.

- **Input**: 描述某个单子已经发起了流程（比如修复 bug 的单子）
- **Expressions**: I already opened a ticket / There's a ticket in for it / I filed a bug on that / It's already in the system / I've kicked off the process / It's been routed to XX
- **Register**: neutral; all six are standard US workplace speech, none of them casual slang
- **Examples**:
  - I already opened a ticket, it's sitting with the platform team.
  - There's a ticket in for it, so we're covered.
  - I filed a bug on that last week, still waiting on a fix.
  - It's already in the system, so it won't fall through the cracks.
  - I've kicked off the process, the fix should land in the next release.
  - It's been routed to the on-call engineer.
- **Avoid**: 「The ticket has been initiated」 - 发起流程 pulled apart into a passive; no native speaker says a ticket was initiated. 「I have already launched a ticket」 - launch does not collocate with ticket, it takes product, release, campaign. 「The ticket is already sent out」 - vague and non-native. 「I started the ticket's process」 - possessive plus process, a calque. 「The bug ticket has been opened by me」 - passive overcorrection, sounds like legal testimony.
- **Notes**: Verb collocations for creating a ticket are open, file, raise, log, put in, write up. Never launch, initiate or start up. 「Raise a ticket」 is BrE and ITIL register, while AmE tech leans on 「open a ticket」 and 「file a ticket」. 「Ticket in」 means submitted: 「there's a ticket in for it」. 「Kicked off」 collocates with process, project and rollout, not with ticket. 「In the system」, 「in the backlog」 and 「in the queue」 describe state rather than creation. 「Routed to」 means ownership moved to the team that fixes it. 单子 itself shifts with context: ticket for a bug or support issue, order for a purchase, work order for maintenance.
