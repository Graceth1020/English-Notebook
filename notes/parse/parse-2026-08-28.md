- Notes: Sentence type: declarative; spoken, with double that-omission and a comma splice before "of course".
  Double stacked object clauses with "that" dropped twice: "I think [that] it's fairly clear [that] there's no such thing...". Very natural in speech; in writing, restoring at least the second "that" improves readability.
  "it's fairly clear (that) ...": extraposition again - "it" is a placeholder and the real subject is the following clause.
  "fairly": a downtoner, weaker than "very" and weaker than "quite" in most US usage - it hedges the claim ("clear enough, not beyond doubt").
  "in tech": ellipsis of "in the tech industry"; "tech" is used as an uncountable domain noun with no article.
  "there's no such thing as X": fixed existential idiom for denying that something exists as a category. "as" is obligatory, and the complement is a noun or a gerund - "no such thing as cutting yourself", never "no such thing as to cut yourself".
  "cutting yourself": gerund after the preposition "as". The reflexive "yourself" pairs with generic "you" (anyone), so it means "cutting one's own costs/staff", the business sense of "cut" (cost-cutting, layoffs).
  "to be a winner": infinitive of purpose = "in order to become a winner". The whole idiom means "you cannot win purely by cost-cutting".
  "of course": parenthetical comma-bracketed marker that concedes the counterpoint before it is stated.
  PUNCTUATION: "of course, balance sheets are ..." is spliced onto the previous clause. Standard writing needs a semicolon or full stop: "... to be a winner. Of course, balance sheets are very important ...".
  "balance sheets" and "P&Ls" are bare plurals with generic reference, so no article. "P&L" = profit and loss statement; the plural is written "P&Ls" (no apostrophe).
  Repetition of "are very important" is rhetorical parallelism for emphasis; in writing it would usually be compressed: "balance sheets and P&Ls are both very important".
